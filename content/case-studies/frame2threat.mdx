## The claim

Richer data does not automatically mean a better model, and the gap between those two things is where
a lot of engineering effort quietly disappears.

I had event data for a possession-danger model, and I had StatsBomb 360 freeze frames: the positions
of the other players on the pitch at the moment of each event. That is materially more information. It
is also more expensive to obtain, store, join and maintain. Adding 41 features derived from it moved
test ROC AUC from 0.8807 to 0.8822, a gain of 0.0014. Precision-recall AUC moved by the same order,
0.8911 to 0.8925.

The claim is that this result should be published rather than buried, because the counterfactual, a
write-up that says "I incorporated 360 freeze-frame data", is technically true, sounds impressive, and
tells the reader nothing about whether it was worth doing.

## Why the obvious approach fails

The obvious approach is to add the richer feature set, observe that the metric went up, and report
that the richer data improved the model. Both halves of that sentence are true here. It is still
misleading.

It fails because the size of the improvement is the entire question. A gain of 0.0014 AUC is
indistinguishable from noise at most sample sizes, and it certainly does not justify a data dependency
that costs you an order of magnitude more in storage and join complexity. Reporting direction without
magnitude turns an engineering decision into a marketing sentence.

It also fails because nobody audits feature sets after the fact. Once "uses 360 data" is in the
description, it stays there, and the pipeline that produces those 41 features has to be maintained
forever. The cost is recurring and the benefit was measured once. This is how models accumulate
expensive appendages that no one can justify removing, because no one recorded what they bought in the
first place.

## What I did instead

I ran the comparison as an ablation rather than an addition. Same model, same split, same target: one
fit on event features only, one on event features plus the 41 derived from freeze frames. That gives a
number for what the 360 data bought, in isolation, rather than a number for how good the final model
is.

The ablation is the cheap part and it is routinely skipped. Most feature work is evaluated by
comparing the new model to whatever came before, which conflates the new features with every other
change made in the same iteration. Holding everything else fixed is the only way the resulting number
means anything.

I kept the losing configuration in the results file. The event-only run is still there next to the
event-plus-360 run, which is what makes this post possible at all: the evidence that the expensive
features did not pay is committed, not reconstructed from memory.

## The result, next to its benchmark

The benchmark here is the event-only model. That is the right comparison, because the question is not
"is this model good" but "did the extra data earn its place".

| Feature set | Test ROC AUC | Test PR AUC |
|---|---|---|
| Event only | 0.8807 | 0.8911 |
| Event + 360 freeze frames (41 extra features) | 0.8822 | 0.8925 |
| Difference | +0.0014 | +0.0014 |

For context on the ceiling, the wider project's possession-level models score considerably higher: a
GRU on possession sequences reaches 0.9524 test ROC AUC and an ensemble with XGBoost reaches 0.965.
Those are a different framing of the problem, not the same task with better features, so they are not
the benchmark for this question. Mixing them in would be exactly the conflation this post is arguing
against.

## What I would not claim

- I do not claim that freeze-frame data is useless. I claim that the 41 features I derived from it
  bought 0.0014 AUC on this target, which is a narrower statement.
- I have not tested whether that difference is statistically significant. On this sample it should be
  assumed not to be.
- The higher possession-level figures (0.9524, 0.965) are a different task framing and are not evidence
  about the 360 features either way.
- I do not present this as solo, hand-written, end-to-end implementation. What I stand behind is the
  ablation design and the numbers it produced, both of which are reproducible from the committed
  results file.
