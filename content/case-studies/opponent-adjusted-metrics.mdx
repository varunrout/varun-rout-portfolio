## The claim

I built a contextual expected-goals model and evaluated it against StatsBomb's own xG on exactly the
same shots. Mine scored 0.80920 ROC AUC. Theirs scored 0.81957. I lost by 0.01037.

That number is on my portfolio, next to the model, in the same size type. The argument of this post is
that publishing it is the correct decision, and that a portfolio full of metrics with nothing beside
them is not evidence of anything at all.

The reasoning is simple. An AUC of 0.809 sounds good. It sounds good because most readers have no
reference point for what a shot-quality model should score, and 0.8 is comfortably above the 0.5 of a
coin flip. Put StatsBomb's number next to it and the reader immediately knows two things I would
otherwise have to be trusted on: that the metric is being computed sanely, and roughly where the
ceiling is. The benchmark is doing the reader's verification for them.

## Why the obvious approach fails

The obvious approach is to report the metric alone. It is the default in almost every portfolio and
most blog posts, and it fails because an isolated metric is uninterpretable in both directions.

It cannot demonstrate success, because the reader has no scale. Is 0.809 good for expected goals? For
a churn model that would be respectable. For a fraud model it would be poor. For shot quality, where
the outcome is dominated by shot location and finishing is close to a coin flip conditional on
location, the reachable range is narrower than people expect. Without a reference the number carries
no information.

It also cannot demonstrate failure, which is the more dangerous half. A model that quietly has a
target leak, or is evaluated on a split that overlaps its training data, will often post a very good
isolated number. Nothing about a lone 0.95 tells you whether you have built something excellent or
made an evaluation mistake. Comparing to a mature external model on identical rows is one of the
cheapest sanity checks available, and it catches the class of error that unit tests do not.

There is a second failure specific to portfolios. If every number on the page is favourable, a
technical reader assumes selection: that the unfavourable ones were quietly not reported. One honest
loss does more for the credibility of the other numbers than another win would.

## What I did instead

I evaluated on the same 15,623 shots StatsBomb had already scored, so the comparison is like for like
rather than across different samples or filters. The models are scikit-learn estimators, deliberately
simple, because the point of the exercise was the contextual features rather than the estimator.

I also reported calibration alongside discrimination. The CxG model's Brier score is 0.07325.
Discrimination asks whether the model ranks shots correctly; calibration asks whether a shot the model
calls 0.2 actually goes in about a fifth of the time. A model can rank well and still be systematically
overconfident, and for anything downstream, aggregating expected goals over a match or a season, the
calibration is what matters. Reporting AUC alone would have hidden that dimension entirely.

The decision I want to flag is that I did not tune against the benchmark. It would have been easy to
iterate on features until the gap closed, and the resulting number would have been meaningless: at
that point StatsBomb's model is functioning as a validation set and the comparison is no longer
independent. The gap is reported from the first honest evaluation.

## The result, next to its benchmark

On 15,623 shots, open StatsBomb data:

| Model | ROC AUC | Brier |
|---|---|---|
| StatsBomb xG (incumbent) | 0.81957 | not reported here |
| My CxG | 0.80920 | 0.07325 |

The gap is 0.01037 ROC AUC in StatsBomb's favour.

What that gap does mean: their model is better at ordering shots by the probability of a goal than
mine is, on this sample. It is a commercial model with more data, more features and years of
iteration behind it, and losing to it by a hundredth of an AUC point is the expected outcome.

What it does not mean: that the gap is large in practice, or that it is even reliably a gap. A 0.01
difference in AUC on a sample of this size is small enough that the ordering could plausibly change on
another sample, and I have not published a confidence interval for the difference. Anyone treating
0.01037 as a precisely measured quantity is over-reading it, including me.

## What I would not claim

- I do not claim to have beaten StatsBomb. I did not.
- I do not claim the 0.01037 gap is statistically distinguishable from zero. I have not computed a
  confidence interval on the difference, so the honest statement is that the two are close and mine is
  behind.
- The model is not the tech headline it might appear to be. These are scikit-learn estimators, and I
  do not describe this work as gradient-boosting or LightGBM based.
- This is open StatsBomb data, not proprietary tracking data, and the result does not transfer
  automatically to other competitions or seasons.
